Architecture paper

Tessera: a permission-masked point service

A pannable, zoomable map over a document corpus in which what a viewer is permitted to see determines not only which items they retrieve, but every count, density, cluster and summary they are shown. Two readers of the same map, holding different clearances, see two internally consistent worlds — and neither can infer the other's from the numbers on screen. The interesting engineering is not the scatterplot; it is the access control that survives contact with aggregation.

Status. The architecture described here is specified in full. Phase 0 — the measurement phase — is complete: its numbers were obtained by running synthetic access-control policies over a real corpus of 2.42 million arXiv papers, scaled to 109 points. The policies are synthetic because no access-labelled corpus of this size is publicly available; where a figure in this paper depends on the shape of a policy rather than the shape of the data, it is marked as such. Phase 1, the implementation, is in progress. Nothing here describes a deployed production system.

Part I

The empty quadrant

1 · Three viewers, one viewport

A map over a document corpus places one mark per document, and is useful precisely because it is too dense for anyone to read the documents behind it. What it shows instead are quantities derived from them: how many marks fall in the viewport, where they bunch, which bunches are big enough to deserve a label, how the density falls away at the edges.

Now give the corpus an access-control policy — some documents are open, some belong to a compartment, some to a case file — and the difficulty appears immediately. Two people open the same viewport at the same zoom. Their permissions differ. The set of documents a particular viewer may see is their visible set, written M_auth. The obvious requirement is that the marks outside M_auth should not be drawn. The requirement that matters is stronger: every quantity derived from the marks must be derived from inside M_auth as well — the count, the density shading, the existence of a cluster, the size printed beside it. And all of it has to happen while the viewer pans, at the speed of a pan.

Figure 1 holds the corpus, the viewport and the zoom fixed and varies only the viewer. Four things change at once, and the simultaneity is the point.

2 · What actually reaches the screen

It is worth settling early what the size of the corpus does and does not demand, because the answer decides what this paper is comparing. A screen has a mark budget. In this design a tile's sample is defined as the k lowest-priority items in that tile inside the governing mask, where k is the on-screen mark budget per tile; the specification's worked example uses k = 30 over tiles of 104 rows. The budget is bounded and configurable, and the selection happens after masking rather than before it. A viewport resolves to a few hundred tiles, so at that budget what arrives in the browser is marks in the thousands — an illustrative figure obtained by multiplying the two, not one the specification states. Nobody draws a billion marks, and nothing here requires it.

The hard part sits behind those few thousand marks. Every count, every density value and every decision about which cluster is substantial enough to gate a label must be exact, must be computed inside the viewer's visible set, and must be recomputed over a fresh set of tiles each time the viewer pans or zooms. Exact counts per tile are what drive the density underlay and the level of detail: without them a tile holding twelve visible items and one holding four million render identically. Sampling after masking is what stops a sparsely authorised viewer from receiving a nearly empty screen while thousands of authorised items sit invisible beneath a sample that selected around them.

Four requirements follow. Three of them the specification states directly. Scale first: "the system holds on the order of 107 points today, with a design target of 109." Then the shape of the predicate: under the reference authorisation plugin, "a principal's auth data resolves to roughly 104 categories and is effectively unique per principal" — the same sentence adds that "term counts are recorded in §16 as unmeasured", so the figure describes the grants a principal carries and not how many index terms they resolve to, a qualification that applies wherever the number appears below. It is uniqueness rather than size that does the damage: it forbids sharing work between viewers, since there is no permission class to precompute an artefact for and no cache entry two viewers will both hit. Then containment: "the access control requirement is hard: data the presented credentials do not satisfy must never be transmitted, in any form, including in aggregate. This rules out serving unfiltered tiles for client-side filtering. Residual channels accepted rather than closed are recorded in Appendix C."

The fourth is not a sentence in the specification but a property of the interaction the other three have to survive. The view is pannable and zoomable, so a pan produces a fresh set of tiles, and that set has to be counted, gated and sampled quickly enough for the map to feel continuous under the hand. The design's own expectation — "low single-digit milliseconds per viewport" warm — is a design expectation rather than a stated requirement, and it is quoted here as one. The reason this fourth requirement is hard is entirely the first three: a viewer's set that is rebuilt per query rather than materialised once and reused pays its build cost on every frame, and with predicates effectively unique per principal there is no cache from which a second viewer could recover it.

Containment is a requirement of the specification and a property of the design, but it is not an absolute one: it is bounded by an enumerated set of residual channels, and the leak register is the honest statement of where it stops. The reason this system exists is not the register. It is that no system surveyed holds all four requirements together at the design target — the nearest miss, discussed in chapter 5, has the security model right and buys the counts only by visiting every cell — and had one held all four, it would have been used.

The claim is narrower than it may look, and the narrowing is worth making before the comparison rather than after it. It is not that nothing else can serve a permission-masked map. It is that three requirements taken together close the options off: the 109 design target, predicates that are effectively unique per principal, and exact per-tile masked counts recomputed on every pan. At 107 points — which, per the specification, is where this system actually is today — the picture is different. An Elasticsearch deployment, whose per-mask memory cost is survivable at that size and whose ordinary aggregates come out masked, or a PostgreSQL build using bitmap primitives rather than a row-level-security policy, would probably be adequate there. It is the design target that forces the architecture the rest of this paper describes.

3 · Where the field draws the line

Systems with per-document security almost universally draw their boundary at retrieval. Records the viewer may not read are removed from the result set; quantities computed over those same records are not. Several vendors document this as behaviour rather than as a defect. Among the Lucene-family engines surveyed for this paper — Lucene itself, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, Vespa, Tantivy, Quickwit and Solr — a built-in per-document security feature exists only in the Elasticsearch and OpenSearch pair, which ship variants of the same document-level security mechanism, and Elastic's own limitations page states that a user holding document-level security "could still... count how many inaccessible documents contain a given term," with scoring using corpus-global statistics that ignore the role query. Solr goes further: domain.excludeTags is a supported, documented feature for computing facet counts outside the security filter. Vespa and Quickwit have no per-document access control at all, so for them the boundary does not arise.

Relational systems fare no better once the plan is visible. A fully patched PostgreSQL with row-level security discloses the exact count of excluded rows through EXPLAIN ANALYZE, which reports Rows Removed by Filter: 99000. Measurement for this project found a plain, unprivileged EXPLAIN disclosing the density of an invisible cluster at roughly 1,500× above background — a viewport at x=[70,71) estimated at 4,748 rows where 50 were visible, against estimates of three to six elsewhere in the same table — because planner statistics are computed over all rows and the comparison operators in a viewport query are leakproof. PostgreSQL's own documentation concedes the position: a user can see the plan, can measure run time, and "might be able to infer something about the amount of unseen data, or even gain some information about the data distribution or most common values," concluding that "if these types of 'covert channel' attacks are of concern, it is probably unwise to grant any access to the data at all." Elsewhere the pattern repeats: BigQuery documents that "side channels, such as the query duration, can leak information about rows that are at the edge of a storage shard"; Snowflake is the one vendor to state the correct semantics explicitly, documenting that statistics-based optimisations are not applicable under a row access policy.

The clean exception across the survey is Apache Accumulo, whose default read path stacks every aggregating iterator structurally above the visibility filter, so an aggregate cannot observe what the filter removed. Its single real leak vector, Summaries, computes label-occurrence counts per key range without filtering by the caller's authorisations — but it sits behind TablePermission.GET_SUMMARIES, a distinct permission not implied by READ and not granted by default. The property comes from where the aggregation sits relative to the filter, not from the strength of the filter, which is the same construction Figure 2 draws on the right.

The result is old. Büttcher and Clarke showed at USENIX FAST '05 that post-filtering search results lets an attacker infer the content of unreadable files from the relevance scores or the relative ranks — plant test files, watch the ranking shift, recover corpus term statistics. Microsoft's own SharePoint documentation recommends pre-trimming over post-trimming specifically because post-trimming leaks refiner counts and hit counts. Kenthapadi, Mishra and Nissim proved at PODS 2005 that a refusal to answer is itself a leak channel whenever the refusal decision depends on data the requester cannot see. That result is a hazard this design has to clear, not a warrant for anything it does, and it is worth separating the two claims it is usually asked to carry at once. Why the system refuses a label it cannot justify from inside the visible set, rather than quietly regenerating a filtered one, is a choice between the Truman and non-Truman models in Rizvi, Mendelzon, Sudarshan and Roy's sense: a Truman system rewrites the request against the authorised view and answers something distorted, which is what row-level security does everywhere it is deployed; a non-Truman system rejects what it cannot answer honestly. Why refusing is nevertheless safe here is the PODS paper's own criterion, simulatability. The containment test is a function of the label's generating set and the requester's own visible set, both of which sit on the requester's side of the trust boundary, so a requester could compute the refusal for themselves and learns nothing from being told it — and because only labels the requester already satisfies are ever evaluated, a refusal cannot betray the existence of a label they cannot see.

4 · The four requirements against the field

Set the aggregate boundary aside for a moment and ask only what has been demonstrated. deepscatter's tagline is "scales over a billion points"; its README's worked examples are 5.5 million tweets and 20 million biomedical abstracts, and its billion-point artefact is a static star catalogue with no masking. Nomic Atlas's marketing says "billions"; its demonstrated scale is 11 million points, the Obelics map, and its own whitepaper states that Nomic "is unable to disclose many technical details of the Atlas system at this time." Apple's Embedding Atlas is more disciplined, publishing benchmarks on stated hardware — up to 4 million points at 60fps and more than 10 million at 25fps on an M1 Pro at 1600×1600 — with an explicitly brute-force philosophy and no server-side filtering seam. Where a surveyed system does reach 109 interactively it does so by aggregating the individual point away: Falcon into summed-area-table bins, Mosaic into density rasters and hex binning. A bin cannot be selected, labelled, retrieved or masked, because there is no longer anything there to mask.

On access-control granularity the finding is blunter, and is quoted here as the survey states it: row-level access control across the entire visualisation category: zero systems. Not one. The granularity ceiling is universally the dataset. The Mapbox Vector Tile specification offers no security seam at all — grepping the 2.1 spec for security, auth, user, permission, access and encrypt returns zero hits — so every per-viewer difference must live outside the tile format. Among vector databases, not one of the six surveyed caches a per-user filter bitset across queries, and every multi-tenancy model is one namespace per tenant rather than a per-row access control list. 5 · Why not Elasticsearch, PostgreSQL or Accumulo?

These three are the closest fits, and none of them is dismissed here on a principle. PostgreSQL row-level security is the most familiar answer, and the finding is an indictment of row-level security as applied to this query shape rather than of PostgreSQL. Because the spatial containment operator <@(point,box) is not marked leakproof, the planner may not use it as an index condition ahead of the security qual, and the spatial index is abandoned entirely; the best row-level-security variant measured for this paper — an EXISTS policy — became a sequential scan plus a subplan executed 107 times, 86,803 ms against a 26 ms baseline, a ~3,340× regression at 107 rows, with two other policy formulations failing to complete in 120 s and 180 s. Separately, and independently of performance, EXPLAIN discloses the excluded-row count and the density of an invisible cluster. Much of this design could nevertheless be built inside PostgreSQL without using a policy at all: pg_roaringbitmap supplies the needed primitives, including selection of the lowest k members restricted to a value range in a single call. What one meets instead is the absence of a live, session-scoped object to hold the mask in, so it is re-deserialised on every query rather than materialised once and reused — at which point the exercise has become rebuilding this design on a less suitable substrate.

Elasticsearch is the closest thing to a drop-in answer for the access control itself, and its document-level security is better than a summary of the field's habits would suggest. Enforcement is a LeafReader wrapper rather than a query rewrite: DocumentSubsetReader is "a reader that only exposes documents via getLiveDocs() that matches with the provided role query", and because enforcement sits at getLiveDocs(), search, _count and aggregations all see the filtered view. A per-tile document count taken under document-level security is therefore correctly masked. That is architecturally the same move as this design's mask, and it is worth saying plainly rather than letting the category's reputation stand in for the engine.

The objection is the cost of the mask and the impossibility of reusing it. The representation is a dense, uncompressed bitset over the whole document space, held on heap, one per distinct role query; Elastic's own bitset-cache javadoc reasons that "an index with 1 billion documents will use more than 100Mb of memory per DLS query", which the survey puts at roughly 125 MB per mask per shard at 109. The cache is keyed on the index and the query together, so predicates effectively unique per principal get no reuse from one another — and the same javadoc anticipates exactly that: "Because DLS supports templating queries based on user metadata, there may be many distinct queries in use for each index, even if there is only a single active role." A workload whose predicates are unique per principal is the pathological case for that cache by construction. What such a cache costs in practice is visible in issue #46817, where a 30 ms query took 26 s for a document-level-security-filtered user; the cause reported there is structural but specific — the reader wrapper is applied during the can_match pre-filter phase, so bitsets are built for shards can_match is about to discard — rather than a general statement about masked query cost. At 107 with modest concurrency the memory position is survivable, which is part of why chapter 2's claim is conditional on the design target.

A residual disclosure channel remains, and it is narrower than a wrong tile count. From the same limitations page, a restricted user "could still learn about field names and terms that only exist in inaccessible documents, and count how many inaccessible documents contain a given term", and "Document level security doesn't affect global index statistics that relevancy scoring uses." Those are term statistics, field-name existence and scoring statistics — not the per-tile counts and densities this system serves, which come out masked. But the specification's containment requirement admits no aggregate over unauthorised data at all, and a term count over inaccessible documents is one. It is an enumerated exception in Elasticsearch and would have to become an entry in this design's leak register — which is precisely the class of thing that register exists to hold to a fixed, enumerated list.

Apache Accumulo is the one surveyed system whose security model is structurally right, and the paper says so plainly: its per-cell security labels are boolean expressions over tokens using conjunction and disjunction with no negation — structurally identical to this design's predicate language — and because the visibility filter is constructed below every aggregation path, an aggregating iterator physically cannot observe a cell the caller's authorisations do not admit. It does not have the aggregate leak.

What it does not have is any index over visibility. There is no way to count the visible cells in a range without visiting each one: no per-block visibility bloom filter, no term-to-cell posting list, no bitmap, and the visibility component of a key sorts after the row, column family and column qualifier, so it can never let a range scan skip a block. On top of that sits a fixed setup cost, because the caller's whole authorisation set is re-sent on every scan and re-parsed on receipt rather than held in parsed form — the survey measures the deserialisation alone at 3.6 ms for 104 authorisations and 43 ms for 105, and puts the total at "~8–10 ms of fixed setup per viewport query before a single cell is read". Separately and independently, Accumulo's sampling is applied before the visibility filter rather than after it, which conflicts with the invariant that sampling happens inside the mask, and would have to be solved on its own terms.

The survey's own verdict on Accumulo as a backend is no, on three grounds it calls each fatal alone: no exact count of visible cells in a range without visiting every cell, sampling before the filter rather than after, and latency three to four orders of magnitude too high. This paper reads the first of the three more narrowly than its source does, and the departure should be visible rather than smuggled. Visiting every cell in a range is a resource cost, and a resource cost can in principle be bought back with hardware, where the sampling ordering and the per-scan setup cost cannot. How much hardware is not something the sources answer, and no figure is offered here: the 0.17–0.50 ms averages in Accumulo's published benchmarking are random single-row lookups on 300- to 1,000-node clusters, which do not bound a visibility-filtered range scan over a tile, and nothing in the survey measures that operation. What the survey does report for a realistic mixed workload is 250 ms for small random lookups under contention with long scans, improving to 5 ms with a scan prioritiser. So the honest statement is that Accumulo's counting cost is quantitative rather than structural, that this is a milder reading than the survey's, and that the two structural objections stand under either reading.

6 · What this comparison does not establish

Two qualifications belong here rather than in a later part, because they cut against the argument just made. The first is about the evidence for the other systems. The prior-art survey behind this chapter disclaims its own absolute numbers: the PostgreSQL and Accumulo timing figures are single-run microbenchmarks on shared hardware, not controlled benchmarks — the ratios are robust, the absolute numbers are not. The 3,340× regression and the 86,803 ms should be read as the shape of a result, not as a specification of what those systems would do on chosen hardware, and no argument in this paper rests on a single mixed-workload latency figure.

The second is about the evidence for this design. Its own headline figure is a counting microbenchmark, not end-to-end serving. Nobody has built the request path yet — Phase 1 is in progress — so a fair end-to-end comparison between this construction and Elasticsearch, PostgreSQL or Accumulo does not currently exist, and the comparison in Figure 2b is a comparison of requirements against published evidence rather than of one running system against another.

The unoccupied corner is the subject of this paper. The rest of it describes a construction that puts a system there: geometry stored in Morton order so that a tile is a contiguous range of row identifiers, a viewer's visible set materialised once per session as a Roaring bitmap and reused for every subsequent viewport, and each count, density, cluster and label computed as bitmap arithmetic over the intersection of the two.

Part II

The shape of the data

Two ideas carry the rest of this paper. An item is numbered twice, in two coordinate systems, for two different reasons; and the cost of what the service does is governed not by how many items are involved but by how they are laid out. Neither is novel alone. What is load-bearing is building the whole system on both at once.

4. Two coordinate systems

Every item has two questions asked of it, constantly and independently. May this viewer see it? And where does it sit on the map? The two want incompatible numbering schemes, and serving both from one numbering is how this kind of system usually goes wrong.

Permission data wants a permanent name. A grant, a suppression, a compartment membership, a deletion deny — each must survive rebuilds and compaction and mean the same thing in every temporal slice of the corpus. So every item gets a permanent entity ID: stable across temporal slices and rebuilds, never reused, never exposed outside the trust boundary. Every permission fact in the system is expressed in this space and nowhere else. Allocation is in append-only batches, so the numbering only grows at the end; with deletions in the mix the gap between the highest entity ID and the number of items that exist widens over time, which is a genuine long-term exhaustion concern.

Geometry wants the opposite: a numbering that reflects where an item is, so that items near each other on screen are near each other in the numbering. But position is not stable — a slice holding different items has a different spatial ordering — so a numbering derived from geometry would change out from under every permission fact ever recorded. Geometry therefore gets its own space. Each temporal slice separately assigns row IDs by spatial rank, and those row IDs are the array indices of the stored columns.

An explicit permutation relates the two. A slice stores a u32 array mapping entity ID to row ID, sized by the maximum live entity ID rather than by item count, with a sentinel for entities not present in that slice. The inverse is not stored separately: it is the entity-ID column of the row-ordered store, which is already there.

That independence is the point of the factoring, and it pays twice.

It pays first in storage. Because the term index is expressed in entity space, it is not duplicated per slice: within a partition there is exactly one index, shared across every slice, and a mask fragment is built once there and permuted into a particular slice's row space on demand.

It pays second, and more sharply, in compression. Row space is fixed by geometry; entity space is free within each append-only batch, because the order in which a batch is numbered is arbitrary — and an arbitrary choice is a resource. Tessera spends it by assigning entity IDs within a batch sorted by term signature, so items sharing the same set of access terms end up adjacent. Term postings then form long runs inside each batch's ID range, and head terms encode as run containers. In the design's own words, "it costs nothing, does not weaken I9, and is safe only because of I10" — safe only because entity IDs never cross the trust boundary, so an ordering that encodes something about an item's permissions cannot be observed by a client.

The measured gains are large and the counterfactual is flat. Arrival-order allocation gives mean posting run lengths of 1.00–1.26 against a random baseline of 1.000 — nothing at all. Signature sorting, simulated against the Phase 0 corpus, moves posting storage by 8.9× on category-shaped labels and 36.7× on the synthetic hash policy, a cached session fragment by between 6× and 1,614×, and mask union cost at equal coverage by up to 130×. Because entity-ID assignment is permanent by invariant, none of this can be retrofitted: arrival-order allocation would lock in an uncompressed index until a full re-allocation rebuild, which is why the signature-sorted allocator is in the walking skeleton rather than on a later optimisation list.

The factoring has a price that constrains the whole serving design. Permuting a mask from entity space into row space is expensive: 8.8 s for a 69M-item mask at 109. That is fine once per session and fatal per viewport, so the permuted mask is cached per token, slice and pin — if that step ever drifts onto the per-viewport path, the system dies. Even the construction matters: naive iterate-and-insert is slow because the inserts arrive out of order, so the implementation gathers entity_to_row[e] into a flat buffer, radix sorts it, and bulk-constructs.

5. Morton ranking

Row IDs are assigned by spatial rank, and the ranking function is chosen for one specific algebraic property rather than for locality in general.

Coordinates are first quantised onto a 216 × 216 integer grid. The bits of the two coordinates are then interleaved to produce a single Morton code, also called a Z-order code; items are sorted by that code and each item's row ID is its rank in that order. Within a leaf tile the ordering is by display priority rather than by deeper Morton bits, which is what makes level-of-detail sampling cheap later.

The interleaving is worth doing by hand once. Take a 4 × 4 grid, so each coordinate is two bits. Write x as x₁x₀ and y as y₁y₀, and interleave them most-significant first as y₁ x₁ y₀ x₀. The cell at x = 2, y = 1 has x = 10 and y = 01, so its code is 0 1 1 0 — decimal 6. Doing that for all sixteen cells gives:

Read off the four quadrants. The top-left 2 × 2 block holds codes 0–3, the top-right 4–7, the bottom-left 8–11, the bottom-right 12–15. Each quadrant is a block of four consecutive integers, and the leading pair of bits names which quadrant it is. That generalises: each successive pair of bits identifies a quadrant one level further down, so a quadtree tile at any depth is exactly the set of codes sharing a given prefix — one contiguous range of codes, and therefore, since row IDs are the rank in code order, one contiguous range of row IDs. This is the prefix property, and it is why Morton is chosen over Hilbert: Hilbert has better locality, but only Morton has the prefix property, and that single property underpins tile queries, density counts, level-of-detail sampling and sharding alike. A precomputed tile table maps prefixes to rank ranges, so answering "which row IDs are in this tile" is two lookups and no search.

Note the division of labour. A tile's identity is purely geometric — a Morton prefix — while its rank range is per-partition and per-slice. That is what lets independently ranked partitions agree on what a tile is without agreeing on any row ID.

Two qualifications belong here, because the prefix property is easy to over-sell. The first is that a viewport is not a tile: an arbitrary rectangle on screen is covered by a set of tiles, and a single logical tile can itself correspond to several ranges once a partition holds multiple segments, so the interface between the query layer and the store is deliberately typed as tile → set of ranges.

The second is more consequential. Morton ordering makes tiles contiguous; it does not make permission masks contiguous. Phase 0 measured this, comparing the mean run length of a mask in row order against the run length expected of a random mask of the same density. A deliberately structureless control policy returns exactly 1.00, validating the estimator; the most realistic principal shape available sits at 1.03–1.15; category-derived policies reach only 2.32, and the most clustered case measured reaches 5.11 — and those category-derived families are the ones the topic-correlation caveat says are flattered, by up to roughly 5×, so they are upper bounds. The recorded conclusion is blunt: realistic masks are essentially scattered, Morton order buys no compression for any family, and the entity- and row-space serialisations of a mask are within noise of each other. The uniform-scatter assumption the earlier scaling analysis had flagged as its weakest premise turned out to be approximately true. There is no hidden upside — which is why compression is bought in entity space, by the signature-sorted allocation of chapter 4, and nowhere else.

One sizing note. At 109 items the 216 × 216 grid gives 0.23 points per cell, so quantisation collisions are not a constraint; past roughly 4 × 109 the grid must widen and the codes become 64-bit.

6. Roaring, and the cost model

The last structural idea is how the sets themselves are represented. A viewer's visible set, a term's postings, a filter's result are all sets of integers over a bounded universe, and all are stored as Roaring bitmaps: the universe is split into blocks of 216 consecutive integers, and each block is independently encoded as a sorted 16-bit array, a literal bit array, or a list of run-length pairs, whichever is smallest for that block's contents. Those blocks are called containers, and the choice of encoding is per container, not per bitmap.

The per-container choice is what makes a wildly skewed term distribution harmless. A head term whose postings are dense and contiguous becomes run containers; the long tail of rare terms becomes small sorted arrays. The crossover between a sorted array and a literal bit array follows from arithmetic rather than tuning: a bit array for a block is a fixed 8 KB whatever it holds, while a sorted array costs two bytes per member, so the array stops being smaller once the block holds more than a few thousand members. The precise threshold is a property of the Roaring format and its reference implementation rather than of this system — see Chambi, Lemire, Kaser and Godin, "Better bitmap performance with Roaring bitmaps" and the CRoaring implementation — and Tessera inherits it. Below container scale entirely, terms with fewer than a few hundred members are held as plain sorted 32-bit arrays; at the measured vocabulary of 117 million terms, 34.4% are singletons.

Now the headline, which is the single sentence the rest of the design is written against:

Bitmap operations cost O(containers touched), not O(cardinality).

The consequences are not subtle. A term with 100,000 postings spans about 15,259 containers when its members are scattered across a 109 universe — that is every container in the universe, since 109 ÷ 216 is 15,259 — and about 2 containers when they are contiguous. Identical cardinality, roughly 7,500× difference in merge work. Measured at 109, building a viewer's mask at 25% coverage costs either 21.7 ms or 2,885 ms for the same 125.12 MB serialised mask, depending on nothing but posting shape.

The same model explains why the scaling curves are flat. Union cost is linear in containers spanned, and the per-container constant drifts up only about 2× across 400× of scale — 54 ns at 2.42M items, 95 ns at 250M, 114 ns at 109 — which is the memory hierarchy, not the algorithm. Two regimes exist, with constants 13× apart: 114 ns per container where postings are sparse and containers are arrays, against 1.54 µs where they are dense and containers are 8 KB bit arrays. Hence the mask-building kernel needs two algorithms, choosing on container density between an n-way Roaring union and a concatenate-radix-sort-and-bulk-construct pass. Hence, too, vocabulary size is almost free on the authorisation path: mask build barely moves between 10 million and 117 million distinct terms, because union cost depends on the postings granted, not the size of the dictionary. The realistic worst case measured — 104 grants over category-shaped labels — is 588 ms.

Now the consequence that matters for a map. Once a viewer's mask is materialised, answering a viewport is a set of range-cardinality queries over it, and their cost is governed by how many containers the ranges touch. A billion-row viewport is not meaningfully more expensive than an eight-thousand-row one, because both are bounded by the same traversal of the same ~15,000 containers. Cost scales with the containers a viewport's ranges touch — with screen area and mask structure — and not with the size of the corpus behind it. That is the scale answer, and every subsequent decision in this paper is written against it: the on-disk layout, the query path, the sharding, and which aggregates the service is willing to offer at all.

Which brings the necessary limit. This is a counting engine, not an aggregation engine. Counts, densities, existence, membership and samples are O(containers) and effectively free regardless of how many items are involved. Sums, means and percentiles are O(visible items), because the values must be gathered, and no bitmap construction recovers this: per-term partial sums double-count, since an item carries several terms and terms do not partition the corpus. A masked global mean over 108 visible items is a scan, and saying so plainly is better engineering than pretending otherwise. A smaller asymmetry sits in the same family: rank and select over a Roaring bitmap are not constant-time — both cost O(blocks before the position) — so per-tile selections are batched from a computed base rank.

Every figure quoted here comes from the Phase 0 measurements: synthetic access-control policies over a real corpus of 2.42 million arXiv papers, scaled to 109 points, on WSL2 with 12 cores, 39 GB and an RTX 3080 — an environment chosen for memory-mapping and page-cache fidelity rather than latency. The timings are indicative rather than certified; the ratios between them are the durable part.

Part III

Turning permission into arithmetic

The previous part established the two coordinate systems and the cost model. This is where a viewer's grants stop being policy and become a bitmap — built once, cached, and thereafter intersected with everything the map does.

7. The plugin boundary

The engine holds one idea about authorisation and nothing else. An item carries a set of opaque term IDs; a token carries a set of satisfied term IDs; the item is visible to the token if and only if those two sets intersect. Everything about how those term sets are derived sits behind two caller-supplied functions, and the authorisation schema is deliberately out of scope for the service, which never receives policy semantics, only opaque terms.

The two functions are asymmetric by design. terms_of_label(item_label) runs once per item at ingest and must be fast; terms_of_auth(auth_data) runs once per authorisation and may be expensive. Both emit opaque byte strings, which the service interns into IDs in a shared, append-only namespace, so agreement between the two functions is nothing more than byte-equality of canonical descriptors. Beyond the consistency requirement of invariant I5, the plugin author owes four obligations: canonical descriptors, determinism, declared cardinality bounds, and respect for that cost asymmetry.

Each half has a name in the literature worth keeping, because each brings a body of work with it. The data function's normalisation of an item's predicate into terms is boolean expression indexing — indexing a collection of boolean predicates so that, given one assignment, every predicate it satisfies can be found. The auth function's job is partial evaluation producing a residual policy in disjunctive normal form, which is why driving authorisation from a policy engine later is a matter of writing one plugin rather than a redesign. And where a deployment needs compartments — sets of items sharing a required term set, gated by an intersection test — the established name is compartmented / lattice-based mandatory access control; dominance.

The reference plugin makes the shape concrete. Item predicates are written as access expressions in the accumulo-access grammar: tokens combined with & and |, parentheses for grouping, and no negation — so the predicate is monotone, DNF normalisation terminates and the term index is sound. Consistency then reduces to one rule: an item is indexed under its DNF terms and nothing finer, so a conjunctive disjunct is interned as a term in its own right and the item does not appear in the postings of that disjunct's constituent parts.

The risk in that construction is known rather than hypothetical. Fontoura et al., in the boolean expression indexing work published at SIGMOD 2010, measured DNF normalisation over Yahoo's ad-serving expressions and found it infeasible beyond nesting depth 2. Treating that workload as comparable to a corpus of access expressions is this design's judgement and not a claim the paper makes; taken as a warning, the mitigation is plugin-side minting of synthetic terms for deep subexpressions, which keeps terms per item linear in expression size at any nesting depth. Expansion itself was not measured in Phase 0, because here it cannot be — a deployment's predicates are its own knob. Both ends of the conservation law minting trades along were measured instead: satisfied terms per token to 104, terms per item to 1,000.

Both functions ship as wasm32 modules with no WASI imports — instantiation refuses any — so determinism is environmental rather than promised: there is no clock and no ambient capability to reach for. A plugin therefore cannot check expiry, and instead surfaces a credential's not_after for the host to enforce; nor is there online revocation, which is bounded by assertion lifetime instead. Shipping the two functions as separate modules keeps their blast radii apart: rotating trust anchors changes only the auth plugin's hash, which invalidates cached masks, and never the data plugin's, which is a full-reindex event.

Failure is fail-closed in the Non-Truman model sense — refusing rather than silently filtering. A trap or structurally malformed output fails the operation; an empty term list, by contrast, is valid, and means default deny on the label side and a zero-visibility token on the auth side. Declared cardinality bounds only warn: a predicate is a monotone disjunction, so more terms means broader intended visibility, and excluding on a bound would answer “visible to many” with “visible to none”.

What the core does with the resulting term IDs is index them. The term index maps each term to a Roaring bitmap of entity IDs, and Roaring's per-block encoding makes the badly skewed size distribution harmless: head terms become run containers, the long tail small sorted arrays. Mask construction then happens once per distinct auth input, unioning the posting lists of the satisfied terms — per partition the token can reach — into one bitmap in entity space. That union, the term index, label containment, level of detail, filters, partitioning and storage never learn where terms came from; only the derivation did.

That the postings are pre-exploded into an (entity_id, term_id) pair relation and joined as a semi-join is a requirement, not an optimisation. The natural alternative — hold terms as a list column and test containment against the presented set — is roughly three orders of magnitude slower in every implementation measured, because containment operators rebuild a probe structure per row. Even the semi-join is too slow for the authorise path at scale and is reassigned away from it: 3.9–10.3 s at a tiled 249.5M corpus, against a postings union 4–540× faster at 36–530 ms. At the full 109 corpus that union costs 230.6 ms for a category-like head grant set (9 terms, mask cardinality 264.3M) and 588.0 ms for 10,000 randomly drawn category-like grants (41.6% coverage, mask cardinality 416.1M) — the realistic worst case, and inside the 0.14–2 s band projected from the 250M measurements. Every timing in this paper was taken on WSL2 with 12 cores, 39 GB and an RTX 3080; the ratios are the evidence, the absolutes a starting point.

This is the two-stage split the service's API is built around. Authorise resolves the caller's authoritative auth data to satisfied terms, builds the mask and returns an opaque token, once per distinct auth input. Retrieve accepts that token plus a query and returns a rendered payload in single-digit milliseconds, many times per second while a user pans, because it works against a precomputed mask rather than re-deriving authorisation. Masks — though not tokens — are content-addressed on the satisfied term set, the auth plugin's version and the identity of the postings they were built from, so nothing invalidates an entry: a changed input is a different key. The auth data is kept alongside, so an evicted mask is rebuilt rather than failing at an arbitrary moment.

Outward, the boundary is narrower still: entity IDs never cross it at all (I10), and clients receive per-session opaque handles instead.

8. Signature-sorted entity IDs

Union cost is not a function of how many entities a mask contains but of how many Roaring containers the granted posting lists span — containers touched, not cardinality. That single fact makes the ordering of entity IDs an engineering decision rather than an implementation detail, because the ordering determines whether a term's members fall into a few containers or are smeared across all of them.

The measurement is stark. At equal coverage and equal mask cardinality, a label shape whose terms span roughly 37 densely-filled containers beats one whose terms span about 15,259 containers — all of them — at around 6.5 postings per container: 400× fewer container visits, and entity-space contiguity worth up to about 130× on union cost as a result. Cost is close to linear in containers spanned, the per-container constant drifting up only about 2× across 400× of scale, from 54 ns at 2.42M entities to 114 ns at 109.

Entity IDs are allocated in append-only batches, and the order within a batch is a free choice the design spends deliberately: assign IDs sorted by term signature — the set of terms the item carries — so that each term's postings form long runs inside that batch's ID range and head terms encode as run containers rather than scattered arrays. It costs nothing, weakens no invariant, and is safe only because entity IDs never leave the service.

Two results bound the payoff. Assignment in created order buys nothing at all: run lengths of 1.00–1.26 against a 1.000 random baseline. Signature-sorted assignment buys 8.9–36.7× on posting storage and up to 130× on union cost at equal coverage — for category-like labels, total posting storage from 7.7 MB to 0.9 MB; for a hash label shape with 100 terms, from 552.9 MB to 15.1 MB, with the cached 25%-coverage session fragment falling from 303 KB to 0.2 KB. For a surname-like shape it buys nothing measurable: like signature alignment and union speed generally, the headline follows the shape of the labels rather than the size of the corpus. These are floors rather than ceilings — at 2.4M items the mean category group holds 44 entities, so its runs are short; at 109 the groups are roughly 400× longer.

The gain is confined to entity space: the row-space mask — the same visible set permuted into Morton order — stays scattered regardless of how entity IDs were assigned. That is why the kernel carries two union algorithms, a Roaring n-way union costing O(terms × containers spanned) for the dense-bitmap regime and a concatenate-radix-sort-construct path costing O(total postings) for the sparse-array regime.

What makes this more than a tuning parameter is invariant I9:

I9 — Entity IDs are append-only and never reused. Masks and generating sets are sets of entity IDs held in caches with non-zero lifetime; reissuing a deleted item's ID grants the new item every access the old one had.

An ID, once assigned, is that item's ID for the lifetime of the corpus. There is no later pass that renumbers entities for better compression, because renumbering is reuse and reuse is a grant of someone else's access. The compression comes entirely from the ordering, so it ships in the first allocator or not at all: created-order IDs would lock in an uncompressed index until a full re-allocation rebuild of the corpus.

One ordering is specifically forbidden. Assigning entity IDs in Morton order is tempting, since it would make the entity-to-row permutation the identity; it is rejected because it would give permission-space IDs spatial meaning, turning the ID gap between two visible points into an estimate of unauthorised data nearby. Why signature ordering is safe and Morton ordering is not comes down to invariant I10:

I10 — Entity IDs never cross the trust boundary. They are dense and assigned in ingest order, so the gap between two visible IDs is a count of unauthorised items ingested in the same window. Clients receive per-session opaque handles instead.

9. One query, end to end

With the mask built, a viewport request is arithmetic over two bitmaps and a permutation. The specification's retrieve path runs to eleven steps; five carry the load, and they are worth following in order.

One. The viewport arrives: a rectangle at a zoom depth, pinned once to a segment-set version that is then used for the tile table, the columns, the permutation and the candidate lists alike, so that everything in the request sees one consistent geometry. The mask fragment carries its own watermark rather than sharing that pin, because a pin fixes geometry and must never fix authorisation.

Two. The rectangle is decomposed into a few hundred quadtree tiles at the target depth. By the Morton prefix property each tile is a contiguous half-open row range [lo, hi) — this is the whole reason for storing geometry in Morton order, and it is what converts a spatial question into an interval question.

Three. The effective mask is composed in entity space and projected into row space. Composition is M_auth = (M_token \ L) ∪ direct_eval(L), where L is the overlay unioned with every entity at or above the watermark; the frozen mask is read through a view over the memory-mapped file, with no deserialisation and no allocation. Filters narrow it by intersection, never touching authorisation. Projection then gathers entity_to_row[e] into a flat buffer, radix sorts it and bulk-constructs the row-space bitmap — the only point in the system at which the two ID spaces meet, and cached per (token, slice, pin) because it is the one expensive step.

Four. Each tile's row range is intersected with that row-space bitmap. intersect_with_range culls the empty tiles outright. Cost here is in containers, not rows.

Five. range_cardinality gives the exact masked count per tile, with no data file touched at all. Only then does selection happen, and that count is what decides how: at or below the per-tile budget take everything, above it take the lowest-priority visible items, evaluated directly from the mask where coverage is low or through a precomputed candidate list where it is high. Priority is a hash-derived per-point constant and is mask-independent, so the sample is a sample of the visible set rather than the visible portion of a global sample.

What follows is mechanical. The selected row IDs collect into one sorted u32 array, so the gather over the memory-mapped Arrow columns is forward-sequential-with-gaps; row IDs then translate to entity IDs and to per-session opaque handles on the way out. Labels are served separately, gated on M_auth and never on the filtered mask.

Three properties carry this design. Counting and post-mask sampling are both bitmap arithmetic over contiguous ranges, so cost scales with screen area rather than corpus size. Projection is the sole meeting point of the two ID spaces, which makes their separation a type rule rather than a convention. And nothing between mask composition and handle translation reads a column except through a masked row-ID set — which is invariant I2, that every derived quantity be a function of visible data only, in structural form rather than as a discipline.

Part IV

Making a map out of a bitmap

Part III ended with a mask: a Roaring bitmap holding the entity IDs of every item a session is permitted to see. A mask is not yet a map. Between the two lie four questions — which points to draw when there are more of them than pixels, what to call the shapes they form, how a search narrows the view without destroying it, and what happens when some of the data may not share a process with the rest.

10 · Level of detail, and sampling after masking

A viewport resolves to a small set of tiles. Because geometry is stored in Morton order, each tile is a contiguous range of row IDs within a segment, so the query for a tile is an intersection of the mask with that range. The cheapest thing this buys is counting: range_cardinality gives the exact number of visible items in any tile at any zoom without touching point data, so a full per-user density pyramid is a few thousand range-count calls, exact rather than estimated. Those counts are worth spending immediately, because without them a tile holding twelve visible items and a tile holding four million render identically — and being per-user and exact, they disclose nothing the tile query has not already disclosed.

Choosing which points to draw is the harder half, and it is governed by an invariant. I7: “Sampling happens after masking, never before. The sample of an authorised set is not the authorised portion of a global sample. Any level-of-detail step must be defined over the visible set; precomputed unmasked structures may be used only as a fast path with an exact fallback.” Reversed, the consequence is quiet rather than loud: a principal authorised for a small or clustered slice would see a nearly empty screen while thousands of authorised items sat invisible beneath a global sample that had selected around them.

The construction that satisfies I7 by definition is priority sampling. Every item carries a fixed pseudo-random priority derived by hashing its entity ID, and a tile's sample is the k lowest-priority items in that tile inside the governing mask, where k is the on-screen mark budget. Nothing in that definition references unmasked data. It also nests provably: an item among the k lowest-priority visible items in a parent tile is necessarily among the k lowest in whichever child contains it, because the child's visible set is a subset — so a representative does not vanish when the reader zooms in on it. An earlier design selected by rank position using a bit-reversal sequence and lacked that property, so essentially every representative popped on zoom-in. Priority also composes across compartmented partitions, where rank position would not.

Evaluating that definition cheaply is a separate question, with two answers. Candidate lists precompute, per tile node, the top c·k items by priority, unmasked; at query time the list is filtered by the mask and the first k taken. Direct evaluation takes the visible row IDs in the tile's range straight from the mask, reads their priorities, and keeps the k lowest. The tile's exact masked count is known before any selection happens, so the choice costs nothing to make.

They move in opposite directions. A list of width c·k yields about c·k·coverage survivors, so it produces k of them only above coverage 1/c; at c = 4 that is 25%, which — given on the order of 104 grants against 105–106 categories — describes very few principals, and widening helps only linearly. Direct evaluation's cost is bounded by the tile's priority block regardless of coverage, and falls as coverage falls. Below the crossover, descent multiplies work rather than dividing it: at c = 4 and k = 30 over 104-row tiles, reaching k survivors visits about 21 nodes at 5% coverage against roughly 10 pages for direct evaluation, and 5,461 nodes at 0.01% against a single page.

So: below roughly 5% coverage evaluate directly, above it use the list. Both compute the same definition, so I7 holds either way. The Phase 0 measurements settled which is the common case: realistic masks are essentially scattered under Morton order, and at working coverages 12–99% of occupied depth-6 tiles fall below the ~5% crossover — for tail-only principals, essentially all do. Direct evaluation is the main selection route, not a fallback; candidate lists serve the dense cores of head principals. Authorisation does not usefully correlate with position, so there is no hidden upside to wait for.

11 · Labels, clusters, and the containment frontier

Callers supply a cluster hierarchy. Tessera uses it as a tree rather than as a flat partition, and that single choice converts insufficient visibility from suppression into rollup. Each node stores a membership bitmap; the service descends from the root evaluating and_cardinality against the mask, and stops when the visible count falls below a threshold. The set of nodes where the descent stopped is the frontier — the clustering this viewer is shown, coarse where they see little and fine where they see a lot. Nobody gets a blank region; they get a vaguer ancestor. Node geometry — centroid, hull, count — is recomputed per user from masked membership, per I2. The governing threshold, min_visible_members, should be reviewed as a disclosure control: every displayed item is authorised either way, so it bounds not access but a residual structural leak, namely that a node's existence and shape derive from global density.

Labels are supplied by the caller together with their generating sets — the items a label was generated from — and the service gates them. I3: “Labels are served iff their generating set is a subset of M_auth. Never of the filtered selection mask. Evaluated per request, with nothing caching a label decision above the check.” The test is one bitmap operation, and_cardinality(G, M_auth) == |G|. It is the compartment-lattice instance of the derivation axiom from multilevel database security; what appears to be unpublished is applying it to shared, precomputed, generated summaries served to differently-cleared viewers. It suits Part III's term model, because a term is permission-homogeneous: a generating set of the form G = node ∩ postings(T) is satisfied by exactly those principals who satisfy T — no coverage fraction, no threshold to defend. Note the dependency the design attaches to that convenience: it rests entirely on the two authorisation functions agreeing about T, and if they disagreed, every generating set of that form would become unsound at the same instant and the containment test would still return true. The same unverifiability governs the declared sets themselves. Provenance is something the service takes on trust: a label supplied with an optimistic generating set is a disclosure the service will faithfully serve, and the leak register carries it as a high-severity channel accepted as the caller's control rather than closed by the service.

Which set the caller declares is the second question the containment test cannot settle for itself. Where prompts are built from a sample of a node's members rather than from its whole membership, “every item it was generated from” can mean the sample or the full membership, and the two differ materially: the sample-based set is far easier to satisfy and therefore far more available, but protects less. The design settles on the prompt sample. It is honest to provenance — only the sampled documents influenced the label text, so the sample is the generating set under I3's definition — and it is the available choice; the consequence, stated plainly, is that a label can reach a principal who is not cleared for every member of the node it describes, only for every member it was written from. Full-membership declaration remains available per deployment as a strict mode, and which was used is recorded in the bundle manifest's provenance. Whichever is declared, it is then fixed. I8: “A label's generating set is immutable once supplied. Items arriving later are not part of it and must not be added. A label whose node has since grown is stale, not unsafe. Members leaving is an availability problem.” So ingest can never make a label unsafe; deletion, which drops an item from every mask and so fails containment for every principal at once, makes it unavailable.

When containment fails, the service falls through a ladder rather than showing nothing: the full node label, then progressively larger cumulative unions, then single-term labels, then an extractive term list computed at view time over mask ∩ node — exactly the visible items, so that tier always satisfies I3 — and finally no label. That tier carries one constraint of its own, and it is an I2 constraint rather than an I3 one: the background document frequencies it scores terms against must come from a fixed public reference corpus, never from the live corpus, because live frequencies are an aggregate over mostly unauthorised data and they determine which terms are shown — violating I2 silently. Because only satisfied candidates are ever evaluated, a principal never learns that a label they cannot see exists, so the refusal itself carries no information.

Filtering is where the frontier gets interesting, and where the obvious implementation is wrong. The filtered mask is a subset of M_auth, so filtered counts are never higher and descending on them against a fixed threshold stops earlier everywhere: filtering would make the clustering uniformly coarser rather than finer around the hits. The fix is two thresholds with different jobs. min_visible_members, against M_auth, sets the maximum depth and stays a disclosure control that filtering must not relax; a much smaller display threshold, against the filtered mask, decides how far within that bound to descend. I12 states it operationally: “Filters narrow rendering; they never touch authorisation. A filter may reduce which authorised items are drawn. It may never enlarge the authorised set, relax a label's containment test, or permit the cluster frontier to descend below the depth M_auth alone would allow. Operationally: a filter may move the frontier up, never down.”

The frontier recomputes on every filter change, so labels appear, refine and disappear as someone types: debounce, and add hysteresis so a node does not drop out because a match count wobbled by one.

12 · Two masks

The mechanism underneath chapter 11 is that there are two masks, not one. M_auth is what the token permits: it governs label containment, maximum frontier depth, node geometry, and the security boundary. M_sel = M_auth ∧ filter₁ ∧ … ∧ filterₙ is what the query asked for: it governs which points render and the matched counts. Every filter is applied to the second and never to the first.

Collapsing them into a single composed mask is the natural implementation and it dissolves the map. Containment would then be tested against M_auth ∧ text ∧ vector; a generating set contains items that do not match the text query, so containment fails for essentially every label the instant anyone types. A frontier descending on filtered counts would likewise dissolve as the filter narrows, destroying the frame of reference exactly when it is most needed. I3 and I12 prevent both, and there is no leak in preserving labels under filtering: the principal could already see them, and per I12 a filter cannot widen authorisation.

An affordance falls out for free. range_cardinality over both masks on the same range gives matched-and-visible against total-visible, exactly, for two bitmap operations — which supports highlight-in-context rather than removing everything else and leaving the reader staring at twelve points on a blank canvas. Rendering follows the same split: a context layer sampled from M_auth, where coverage is normal, and a match layer from M_sel, small enough under a selective filter to send in full.

What makes this composable is the filter contract: every filter returns a set of entity IDs as a bitmap, and composition is intersection. That is also the extension discipline for the retrieval surface — new filter forms add operands rather than changing the shape of the call. Four rules make it hold. Threshold, never top-k, because a top-k query evaluated alone is computed over the whole corpus, so intersecting afterwards is post-filtering and the principal's nearest neighbours would vary observably with items they cannot see. Optional candidate push-down: filters accept an optional candidate bitmap, which makes vector brute-force viable and removes that post-filter leak in the same move. The mask goes in first, not last, because it is usually the most selective operand and every un-intersected intermediate contains unauthorised IDs. And pre-intersection cardinality is structurally unreachable, because a raw match count is a corpus-wide count over unauthorised records.

It stays a pipeline rather than becoming a planner. Intersection is the only top-level operator, and the order is fixed at design time by cost class: label, then text, then intersection with M_auth, then a brute-force vector scan over the survivors. Statistics-driven reordering is deliberately absent, because it would make execution time a function of how much the principal can see. Two corollaries are easy to miss: the label vocabulary offered for filtering must itself be containment-filtered, or a filterable label reveals a label the principal is not cleared to see; and text filters but does not rank, because relevance scores computed from corpus-global statistics are a demonstrated channel for inferring the content of unreadable documents.

The design point is worth stating plainly. Filters narrow which points are shown without dissolving the map, and however expressive the filter surface becomes, none of that expressiveness reaches the authorisation layer. Every filter is one more operand on the same intersection, and M_auth is not an operand the caller can write.

13 · Compartmented partitions and required-set gating

Masking assumes every item can sit in the same process, distinguished only by a bit in a bitmap. Some terms mark data for which that is not acceptable: it must be held separately at rest and in memory. For those, Tessera uses partitions — a partition being a set of items sharing a required term set, stored in its own files and served by its own process. It is a second sharding axis: by row range for scale, by term for isolation, and the two compose. This is compartmented, lattice-based mandatory access control made structural.

One distinction decides whether the design is valid at all. If the requirement is that data be separated, so that compartment A's items are not co-resident with compartment B's, the design is correct. If it is that audiences be separated, so that A-cleared and B-cleared principals never touch the same hardware, then items spanning compartments must be rejected rather than placed. Tessera takes the first reading.

A partition's required set is the intersection of compartment markers across every disjunct of an item's predicate — every route to the item, not merely one. If an item's terms are {a, R} and {b, R}, R is genuinely required; if they are {a, R} and {b}, R is not, because the second term admits a principal without it. The gate follows: a token may query a partition only if it satisfies every term in the required set. That is exactly sound rather than merely conservative — a token satisfying any of an item's terms necessarily satisfies everything in the intersection, so the gate never excludes an authorised token. The mixed case falls out instead of needing a rule: an item with one uncompartmented disjunct has an empty intersection and lands in the default partition, and any compartment marker on its other disjuncts is provably doing nothing. That is almost certainly a labelling error, so warn loudly — but it is not a security violation, so it must not block ingest.

Everything is keyed on entity, so everything partitions with the items: the term index, the columns, the permutation, the tile table, node membership, generating sets, the text index and the vectors. Each partition has its own Morton ranking and row-ID space, preserving the property that a row ID is an array index. The security consequence is that the mask never exists whole: each partition builds its own fragment from its own term index, so no process outside a compartment holds a bitmap containing its entity IDs. That is the isolation property, and it is why authorisation fans out.

Two of the three quantities compose almost for free. Counts sum, because tile identity is geometric: partitions agree on which tile is which while mapping it to their own rank ranges. Priority sampling composes exactly, because priority is a global per-item property — the k lowest-priority visible items in a tile are the k lowest of the union of each partition's own k lowest. The rank-position scheme rejected in chapter 10 would not have composed at all.

Containment is the one that bites. Generating sets and masks both partition by entity, so G ⊆ M if and only if Gp ⊆ Mp for every partition p — subject to I13, which says that a partition not consulted fails closed: a partition the token cannot reach counts as contributing nothing satisfied, never as vacuously satisfied. A label whose generating set has a non-empty slice in an unreachable compartment must be withheld. The natural implementation, which checks only the partitions it queried, serves labels it should withhold, and does so silently.

Partitions are discovered rather than declared: an item arriving with a previously unseen required set creates one, leaving existing partitions untouched. Identity is a canonical hash of the sorted required set, which makes creation idempotent under racing ingest workers, and creation should raise an alarm, because a new combination means either a data error or a policy change. The count needs a cap: a token fans out to as many as 2n − 1 partitions where n is the number of compartments it holds, and fan-out p99 is the slowest partition.

Isolation strength is a ladder rather than a switch: separate processes with separately mapped files is the cheapest meaningful rung; a separate memory cgroup and no shared page cache closes core dumps and swap; a separate host with its own keys and operator access closes the rest. The compartment map itself, though, is not a runtime knob. An item whose required set changes simply moves stores, but changing policy — compartmenting a previously open term, splitting or merging compartments — means re-evaluating placement for every affected item. That is a reindex, mitigated only by immutable versioned prefixes, which let a repartitioning be built under a new prefix and cut over by flipping a pointer.

These four chapters are what turns a bitmap into something a person can read. The recurring shape is that every quantity on screen — the count in a tile, the point representing a thousand others, the word attached to a cluster — is computed from inside the viewer's own visible set, while in each case the cheap version is computed from something larger. Part V turns to what happens when that visible set changes underneath a reader already looking at it.

Part V — Time and change

14. Ingest: entity space only grows

A real corpus moves: documents arrive, documents are withdrawn, and the clearance attached to a document changes without the document changing at all. Each has to be reconciled with masks computed before it happened. What makes that tractable is a deliberate restriction on what ingest may touch.

Entity space is append-only — invariant I9. Ingest appends entity IDs and appends postings to the term index. It never reorders, never rewrites, and never invalidates anything expressed in entity space: masks, node memberships and generating sets all remain valid, merely incomplete. That distinction is the whole of the design. An invalidated mask must be discarded and rebuilt; an incomplete one has a boundary, and a boundary can be recorded and patched. Target ingest visibility latency is seconds to minutes, and it is not coupled to any credential cadence, because the model has none.

The churn lives in row space instead, where new items interleave arbitrarily into the existing Morton ranking and a correct in-place insert would renumber a large fraction of the slice. That is why the permutation between entity IDs and row positions exists (invariant I4): row order may be rewritten wholesale at a merge, because nothing durable depends on it.

Append-only does not mean order-free. Within a batch, entity IDs are assigned sorted by term signature, so postings form long runs inside the batch's ID range: created-order assignment yields run lengths of 1.00–1.26 against a 1.000 random baseline, which is to say nothing, while signature-sorted assignment buys 8.9–36.7× on posting storage and up to 130× on union cost at equal coverage. Assigning IDs in Morton order instead is prohibited: it would give permission-space IDs spatial meaning, turning the ID gap between two visible points into an estimate of how much unauthorised data lies between them. Signature ordering is safe only because entity IDs never cross the trust boundary (I10) — and because I9 makes assignment permanent, it ships in the first implementation of the allocator or not at all.

The log and the ack

Each partition has one write-ahead log with a single appender, its lifecycle thread; records are append-only, length-prefixed and individually CRC'd. That thread owns every mutation decision but does only cheap work itself — drain the command queue, append and fsync, swap pointers — while everything else runs on a background pool. The queue has a priority lane for deny-disposition changes, so a compaction publish or a stalled object-store upload can never queue a suppression behind seconds of IO: deny visibility latency is bounded by queue-front plus fsync, and nothing else.

The ack ordering is load-bearing: WAL fsync, then the overlay and generation swap, then the 200. The swap takes nanoseconds and sits before the ack, so a caller's own next request always observes its own accepted change; the window it leaves — after fsync, before the swap — recovers by replay and was never acked.

Recovery replays from the last flush marker, and a CRC failure is read by position. In the unsynced tail it truncates the log — those records were never acked. At or below the last fsync point the failure is corruption of acked state, possibly including denies, and recovery fails closed until an operator restores from the bundle and the object store; truncate-at-first-bad-CRC applied mid-log would silently drop acked denies. Exhaustion follows the same principle: the ingest 429 threshold sits strictly below the log's hard bound so change records always have room to land, and if an append genuinely fails the deny is applied to the in-memory overlay and swapped — visible immediately — while the response is a 500 with an alarm. Never a 200 without fsync, never a silent drop, never a refusal that leaves the item visible.

Buffer, watermark, overlay

Arrivals land in an in-memory buffer. A flush policy — size or age, whichever trips first — turns that buffer into an immutable on-disk segment, so segment count is governed by the flush interval rather than by the arrival rate. Every mask fragment carries an entity high-water mark: a fragment built at watermark W is authoritative below W, and entities at or above W are new and not yet folded in. Flushing advances W by OR-ing in the flushed segment's contribution for the token's already-known satisfied terms — a small, monotone patch rather than a rebuild.

Changes to items already below W go to the overlay, which holds the items in flux: those whose predicate changed, those deleted, and those administratively suppressed. Each entry carries a disposition — evaluate, meaning test its current term set against the token's, or deny, meaning invisible regardless — and carries its term set inline. The disposition is what lets one mechanism cover both a predicate change and an administrative suppression. The live set is the overlay together with every entity at or above W, and evaluating it needs no index at all: an item's term set is a handful of IDs, so visibility is a set intersection against the token's satisfied terms — microseconds for tens of thousands of items — and the set stays bounded by the change rate times the interval before masks are naturally rebuilt. A deletion is a tombstone in this frame: remove the entity from the term index, add a deny entry, notify the caller of the labels affected, and drop the row at the next compaction. The ID is never recycled.

Segments, merges, compaction

A tile resolves to one contiguous range per live segment, so query cost is linear in segment count and segment count must be bounded. Merge policy is structured on established lines rather than run as a scheduled job: a floor size, so a tail of tiny segments cannot dominate decisions; a maximum merged-segment size, so no merge becomes an unbounded rewrite; and a separation of the reasons to merge — natural tiering, forced compaction, and tombstone reclamation on a deletes-percentage trigger. Re-ranking is a decorator on that policy rather than a phase of its own — reorder only merges above a minimum document count, always reorder on forced merges — so the Morton re-rank becomes a continuous property of large merges rather than a scheduled cliff. The reference points taken from a widely-deployed policy are ten segments per tier, a 5 GB maximum merged segment, a 2 MB floor, a 20% deletes threshold, and reordering above 218 documents.

Compaction snapshots a generation, folds the snapshot-covered posting deltas, tombstones and evaluate entries into the base postings, rewrites the permutation, tile table, candidate lists and columns, publishes under a new segment-set version, and lets in-flight requests drain. What it must not fold is specified as carefully as what it folds: segments and deltas flushed after the snapshot, tombstones accepted after the snapshot, the active suppression set, and all unfolded overlay entries are carried forward verbatim. That tombstone clause is a correction — an earlier rule covered only segments and deltas, and folding away a post-snapshot tombstone while the entity survives in the folded base is fail-open.

15. Denial: generations, pins, and three retirement rules

All of a partition's serving state hangs off a single atomically swappable pointer to an immutable generation: the segments and their version number, the postings view, the watermark W, and the overlay with its own version number. The two version numbers move for different reasons. The segment-set version moves on flush and compaction — row-space and postings shape. The overlay version moves on every accepted change batch — security state. Every mutation builds a new generation sharing its unchanged parts by reference count; a change-only generation is two small allocations, which is what makes a suppression cheap enough to apply synchronously.

One ordering rule governs how a request reads that state, and it is tested rather than assumed: a request thread loads the generation pointer exactly once, at request start, before acquiring any fragment or cache entry — overlay resolution happens-before fragment acquisition. That is what makes fragment eviction safe while a request still holds a fragment: the request's own overlay still carries any deny the ledger has since retired. An implementation that refreshes a fragment mid-request leaks in the window between eviction and retirement.

Pins

A pin is a counted reference to a generation; superseded generations sit on a drain list until the last holder lets go. The reclaim ordering is deliberate — remove the entry from the drain list, then verify the strong count is one, then reclaim — because verify-then-remove is a use-after-free that review caught. A racing pin resolution that misses the removed entry gets a 410 pin-expired, which is the correct answer.

What a pin does not do is the point:

Pins fix geometry, never authorisation. A pinned request uses the pinned segments_version for tiles, columns and permutation, but composes M_auth against the current overlay — deny entries apply the moment they are accepted, pinned or not.

The consequence is visible to users and is not a bug: “a pinned drill-down can return fewer items than the viewport before it — is correct behaviour.” The composition stays coherent under mixed versions because M_auth is computed entirely in entity space, with the fragment's own watermark defining the live set; projection through the pinned permutation then drops row-absent entities via the sentinel, so there is no gap and no double count. For the same reason, fragments are built on a cache miss from the current generation's postings view and never from a pinned one: a fragment is authorisation state.

The three retirement rules

A deny entry cannot stay in the overlay forever; the overlay would grow without bound. So each entry needs a condition under which it may be dropped — a retirement rule. The design's recorded decision reads:

Three retirement rules, not one (amended r2): deletion denies by the epoch ledger with an insertion floor; suppressions only by unsuppress; evaluate entries at their compaction fold. r1's single rule was fail-open for two of the three.

Deletion denies retire by the epoch ledger. A deletion stamps its deny entry with a tombstone epoch d and applies a delta-tier tombstone at d immediately. The entry may leave the overlay only when no servable fragment epoch predates d. The fragment cache maintains a count of live fragments per postings epoch, whose lowest key is the minimum live epoch, and the lifecycle thread retires the retirable prefix below it after evictions and periodically. The cache additionally tracks a retirement floor — the highest epoch of any retired overlay entry, whether a deletion's d or an evaluate entry's fold epoch f — and refuses to insert any fragment whose epoch is below that floor. Without the floor, a pinned or slow request could rebuild an old-epoch fragment after the entries predating it were retired, and resurrect a deleted item or a revoked term. Refusal is cheap: the builder retries against current postings. The ledger is per worker and in memory, and losing it on restart is safe by construction, because the cache restarts cold.

Suppressions retire only on unsuppress, and never touch postings. The rule leaves no room for interpretation: a suppression is “never — suppression does not touch postings” retired by any epoch, and leaves “only by unsuppress. Non-retirable while active, by construction: no fragment rebuild ever excludes a suppressed entity, so its invisibility rests on the overlay entry for as long as the suppression stands.” Unsuppress removes the entry and publishes a side-manifest immediately, as all deny-state changes do. The obvious objection — that the active-suppression set then grows monotonically — is answered by treating its size as a metric: a growing suppression set is a policy signal, not a leak.

Predicate-change entries retire at their compaction fold. An evaluate entry carries the entity's current term set inline, and retires “not until compaction folds it — deltas cover newly flushed entities only, so a change to an existing entity's terms is invisible to postings in both directions.” Compaction rewrites the affected entities' postings from the term sets carried in their evaluate entries; after the fold the entry carries its fold epoch f and retires under the ledger rule above, with f participating in the retirement floor exactly as a deletion's d does. That symmetry is required, not decorative: a fragment predating the fold misreads the entity in both directions — a revoked term still present is fail-open, a granted term absent is merely wrong counts — so a floor raised only on deletion retirements “passes the deletion test and still fails open through a pre-fold fragment.” Before any fold, evaluate entries are immortal, which is why overlay growth under predicate churn schedules a compaction rather than just a fragment refresh.

An earlier revision had a single rule, and it was a rule about denies: every deny was assigned a retirement epoch. Evaluate entries are not denies, and the rule that governs them arrived only with the amendment quoted above — which is why the recorded verdict is that the single rule was fail-open for two of the three. For deletions it is right, because a deletion leaves a tombstone in the postings, so once every servable fragment postdates it the postings themselves carry the denial and the overlay entry is redundant. For suppressions it is fail-open. A suppression carries no postings-side signal at all — no tombstone, no fold — from which its invisibility can be re-derived, so an epoch-based timer expiring the entry resurrects the suppressed item with no unsuppress having occurred: any epoch eventually retires the entry and re-exposes the item. The third failure is the one the amendment had to generalise for: raising the floor only on deletion retirements leaves a pre-fold fragment insertable after an evaluate entry logically retires. Splitting the rules by disposition is the fix. Stated generally, the row that must never exist is “any path that loses or re-exposes an acked deny”; the ack ordering, the suppression rule, the insertion floor and the post-snapshot tombstone carry-forward each close one such path found in review, and all four carry conformance tests.

Part VI

What was measured, and what is still open

16. The billion-row table

Phase 0 was a rehearsal: synthetic access-control policies run over a real corpus, at the size the design targets. The corpus is the join of two public sources — the arXiv-Topics-Embeddings dataset, 2,422,486 arXiv papers carrying one BGE-large-en-v1.5 topic embedding each, and the Kaggle Cornell-University/arxiv metadata snapshot v296, with 3,113,330 records of identifiers, categories, authors and versions. The join probe returned clean: all 2,422,486 embedding IDs match a snapshot identifier verbatim, with no normalisation needed in either direction. That joined set — 2,422,486 rows, 117 MB — is the base corpus, and nothing downstream reads the raw sources.

Scaling to 109 exploits a property of the design: entity IDs are append-only, so a prefix of entity space is a whole, coherent corpus. The larger scales were built by adding transformed replicas rather than duplicating rows, and the distinction is load-bearing. Naive tiling would make the corpus easier than the base, because identical copies give every term N identical posting blocks and every tile the same occupancy, so both mask structure and spatial structure go degenerate. Each replica is instead an affine transform — rotate, reflect, log-uniform scale 0.02–0.55, translate — plus jitter, with 45% of replicas placed on shared hubs.

The policies over that corpus are synthetic: label generators pinning the distributional knobs real policies would set — realistic skew from categories and surnames, orthogonal controls from a hash, a 361,837-term vocabulary, per-item noise. They were chosen to bracket distributional shapes, not to predict any deployment's. Everything below should be read in that light.

The headline of Figure 15 is that whole-viewport exact masked counting costs 0.1–0.3 ms at every zoom level, including depth 0, where a single call counts the entire billion rows in 191 µs. The reason it cannot blow up at coarse zoom is structural rather than fortunate. Cost is O(containers spanned), and the mask has only about 15,000 containers, so any set of ranges — one tile or three hundred — is bounded by one full traversal of the mask. There is no zoom level at which the counting gets to be expensive.

That corrects a worry the design had carried about the zoomed-out overview. The overview is the expensive request, but the expense is mask materialisation and shard fan-out, not counting.

Which brings the counterweight. In the same measurement run, the permutation of a mask from entity space into row space — the step that makes a mask answerable by contiguous range arithmetic — cost 8.8 seconds for a 69M-item mask. That is four orders of magnitude above the count it serves, and it is the number that governs the architecture around it. It is why the permuted mask is cached per (token, slice, pin) and built once per session rather than once per request, and it makes the consequence of getting that wrong concrete: if the permutation ever drifts onto the per-viewport path, the system dies. A paper reporting only the 191 µs would be describing a system that does not exist. The 8.8 seconds is the cliff the design is built around, and naming it is the only way the 191 µs means anything.

Three further findings from the same run bear on the design rather than on latency. First, realistic masks are essentially scattered under Morton order — a flat-hash control at 1.00, surnames at 1.03–1.15, categories at 1.7–2.32, an archive head at 5.11 — so Morton order buys no Roaring compression for any policy family. The two clustered families are the ones the measurement itself flags as flattered by topic correlation, so those figures are upper bounds rather than expectations. Second, the compression that does exist is not free from arrival order: created-order entity IDs give run lengths of 1.0–1.26, whereas simulated signature-sorted assignment compresses postings by 8.9× for categories to 36.7× at t=100, and a 25%-coverage session fragment by 6× to 1,614×. That is why signature-sorted assignment is implemented from day one rather than retrofitted. Third, I7's exact direct-evaluation route is load-bearing: for tail-only principals essentially every tile falls below the crossover at which sampling would take over, so the standing warning against simplifying that route away is not hypothetical.

Three things were deliberately not measured. DNF expansion under the reference plugin was not, because the predicates are the project's own knob and measuring their expansion would be circular. The true terms-per-item distribution, and with it the cap-adequacy criterion, was left unresolved. And retrieve-path latency was left alone entirely, reserved as Phase 1's exit criterion on certified hardware. The overall verdict was go — nothing measured contradicted an invariant-bearing assumption, and several findings closed escape hatches — but almost every headline is policy-dependent. Signature alignment, posting compression and union speed all turn on the shape of the access labels rather than on corpus size; the same histogram decides all three; author-like policies get none of them and category-like policies get all three. The machinery is validated. A deployment's numbers are not.

17. What is still open

The largest open question is the one the measurements could not reach: how real access labels are shaped. The synthetic policies bracket behaviour, they do not measure it, and the data can say nothing new about how real access-control labels cluster. In an earlier revision this was a gate with go, rework and stop authority attached to a real-label rerun. It is now closed by owner decision, because no real access-labelled corpus is available to this project, and the question converts to deployment guidance: any deployment holding real labels should re-run the Phase 0 measurements before trusting the policy-dependent conclusions. That is a weaker resolution than a measurement and is recorded as such.

Adjacent to it sits term cardinality under the reference plugin — how many distinct terms exist, how many each item carries, what fraction carry more than one. Those three numbers size the index, the ingest cap and the additivity of the histogram machinery, and under the reference plugin they depend on DNF expansion, which published measurement puts as infeasible beyond nesting depth 2 for comparable workloads. Measuring the expansion factor on real predicates therefore comes first. It is where the design fails, if it fails.

Sharded index placement remains open. So does the compartment question — how many compartments exist and how many combinations actually occur — which sets the fan-out bound and the per-partition overhead budget. So do several sizing questions that only a deployment can answer: how many temporal slices must be simultaneously browsable, how many live segments the per-tile range fan-out tolerates before it is noticeable, and whether source embeddings are served at view time at all. One of these is a hard limit rather than a preference: consumed entity IDs exceed live items without bound, and at 109 items the 232 headroom is thin, with columns, permutations and standard Roaring all 32-bit. The likely answer is shard-local u32 identifiers under a (partition, shard, offset) global ID.

Some questions closed in directions worth stating plainly, because a reader might otherwise assume the generous answer. Retroactive revocation across temporal slices was resolved as no: current credentials govern all slices, and the shared-mask premise stands. A viewer whose clearance is withdrawn today loses last year's slice too, and a viewer who gains one gains it retroactively. Separation between compartments was resolved as data separation rather than audience separation. Token lifetime carries a default backstop of one hour, deployment-overridable, with the caller's own re-authorisation cadence remaining the governing policy — at measured mask-build costs the extra authorise load is negligible, and the staleness bound tightens by an order of magnitude over the earlier twelve-hour working assumption.

The last open item is not a question but a table. The invariant that every displayed quantity derives from visible data alone admits known exceptions, and those exceptions are enumerated: C1 to C16, with the channel, what leaks, a severity, a mitigation and a status. The register matters less for any individual row than for the fact that it can be finished. The viewer-facing retrieval surface is about five shapes — a masked count over a range, the k lowest-priority visible items in a range, a label containment test, a region selection and a drill-down — and a surface that small can be walked exhaustively. A system offering a general expression endpoint could not produce this table at all; it could produce a list of channels somebody had thought of. The register is therefore a property of the design rather than an appendix to it, and the sentence that governs it is unambiguous: anything not listed is a bug, not a trade-off.

Tessera began at an empty quadrant: systems that scale interactive cartography over very large corpora do not treat authorisation as part of the aggregate, and systems that reason carefully about authorisation do not usually have to answer a count in a quarter of a millisecond. The claim of this paper is that the two axes are compatible, that the price of compatibility is a narrow query surface and an entity ordering chosen before the first item is ingested, and that the price is worth paying. What has been measured is that the arithmetic holds at 109 points. What has not been measured is how a real institution's labels are shaped, and that is a rerun rather than a redesign — but it is a rerun someone still has to do.
